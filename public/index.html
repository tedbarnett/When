Events are trapped in silos.

What’s happening tonight in your city is scattered across Meetup, Facebook, Eventbrite, venue websites, and a hundred Instagram stories. Each one is a closed destination that holds listings hostage to its own growth. None of them answer the simple question: what’s going on around here?

when.org is the open alternative. The unit isn’t an app or an account — it’s a calendar with an owner. A person who knows every jazz room in New York publishes a calendar, and you subscribe the way you’d subscribe to a newsletter. It shows up in the calendar app you already use, and it stays current.

Feeds are free forever. Reading is free. Publishing is free. Subscribing your calendar app is free. Data out is sacred: every page carries open standards, so nothing you build here is ever locked in.

The platform is free, but curators can sell calendar subscriptions, tickets, tips. Think of it as a Substack for calendars — free, or if it’s good enough, paid.

The people who make the scene should make money.

Curators

Offer a paid tier on your calendar: premium picks, early access, the weekly digest. You keep 90%. Nobody else pays taste-makers for knowing their city.

Hosts

Sell tickets and paid RSVPs natively for about a 1% platform fee plus card processing. The big ticketing platforms charge six times that. On free events, guests can tip you.

Venues

Embed your own calendar on your website free, and claim your venue page so every listing points home. Pro tools for branding and analytics come later.

when.org only earns when you do: a small share of payments, never a paywall on the feed itself.

1998 · Redwood City, California

Before When.org, there was When.com.

The When.com homepage, February 2, 1999 · open full-size ↗

In 1998, When Inc. of Redwood City, California launched When.com — one of the web’s first calendar services. Members tracked personal schedules online and subscribed to live event feeds: concerts, movie openings, TV shows, trade shows, even horoscopes — with automated crawlers refreshing event data around the clock.

In February 1999, Business 2.0 profiled founder Ted Barnett in a piece titled “Calendar Boy,” calling When.com a bid to “create the ultimate sticky app.” Barnett told them: “Right now, we’re not interested in selling. We know we’re onto something big.”

Two months later, on April 5, 1999, America Online acquired When Inc. in a stock deal reported at $225 million. AOL president Bob Pittman called it “the perfect personalization tool,” and When.com became “My Calendar” in AOL 5.0 — the ancestor of AOL Calendar.

Sources: Business 2.0, Feb 1999 · The New York Times, Apr 6, 1999 · Salon, Sep 1999
